01

Context

BTCFi was not a standalone frontend launch. Its customer experience depended on coordinated delivery across assets, oracle and pool setup, cross-chain handling, referral services, existing DeFi products, infrastructure, QA, customer support, and external communication. Pre-launch and production readiness also had to advance in parallel.

02

Problem

A conventional project schedule would show tasks, but it would not make the launch risk legible. The real challenge was coordinating dependencies between teams with different release mechanics while preventing pre-launch work, production changes, and public-facing communication from drifting out of sequence.

  • Multiple launch tracks shared infrastructure and engineering resources.
  • Contract, node, backend, and frontend changes had different lead times and failure modes.
  • User-facing exposure required explicit review before release.
  • QA depth had to match the risk of each product path rather than follow one uniform process.
03

My Role

I owned the integrated launch strategy: translating the product target into milestones, sequencing cross-team dependencies, setting readiness criteria, assigning decision points, and maintaining a shared operating picture for engineering and non-engineering stakeholders. My responsibility was the quality of the whole delivery system, not one implementation layer.

04

Decision Making

I structured decisions around launch risk and reversibility. Critical BTCFi and pre-launch paths used scenario-based test cases, while limited changes to already operating products used targeted functional QA. User-visible changes were reviewed before implementation, and shared testnet infrastructure was used to reduce confusion between parallel launch tracks.

Risk-weighted validation Match QA depth to product novelty and blast radius.
Dependency before date Sequence releases by what must be true, not only by calendar order.
Controlled exposure Align frontend, domains, customer support, and communication before launch.
05

Execution Strategy

The roadmap moved from a portfolio view into an operational runbook. Milestone planning established the dependency graph; release-day planning then converted it into ordered handoffs, readiness checks, and explicit coordination windows.

06

Outcome

The work produced one coordinated path from testnet through pre-launch and production. Teams could see not only their own tasks, but also the dependencies, approval points, and operational handoffs that determined overall readiness. The launch plan became a control surface for product delivery rather than a passive schedule.

Leadership impact

Product ownership for infrastructure-heavy products means designing the execution model: technical sequencing, decision rights, risk controls, and communication all have to work as one system.

07

Lessons Learned

Large blockchain launches fail at interfaces more often than within a single component. A technical leader creates leverage by making those interfaces explicit: who decides, what depends on what, how readiness is proven, and when product communication can safely follow engineering reality.
